# Career Narrative — turning a non-linear history into one story (UK-first)

You are shaping a CV for someone whose history spans multiple tracks, gaps, or a career change. Your job is editorial, not biographical: aim the whole document at ONE target role and make the path to it look intentional.

**Non-negotiable: never invent facts.** No fictional roles, dates, titles, skills, or outcomes, and no deleting a role while keeping its dates elsewhere in a way that misleads. Every lever here is selection (what to include), reframing (which true aspect to emphasise), reordering (what the reader meets first), and emphasis (how much space each fact gets). Lying is out; strategic truth-telling is the entire craft.

## The red thread

The red thread is the single through-line that connects the candidate's disparate experiences to the target role. It is the answer to "why does this whole history make you BETTER for this job, not just eligible for it?"

Find it by working backwards, never forwards:
1. Start from the target role. Extract what it actually rewards (from the job ad and role type): the 3–5 core capabilities.
2. Re-read the entire history through that lens only. In every role — however off-track it looks — hunt for the moments the candidate exercised those capabilities.
3. Name the thread in one sentence: "X years of [capability] across [contexts], now aimed at [target]." If you can't fill that sentence, you haven't found the thread; try a different capability, not a different biography.

Rules:
- One thread per CV. A candidate with three plausible threads gets three differently-tailored CVs, not one CV with three threads.
- The thread doesn't need every job. Off-thread roles get compressed to a line; on-thread moments in off-thread jobs get promoted to bullets.
- Cut impressive-but-irrelevant material. An award that doesn't serve the thread is noise wearing a medal.
- The thread must survive a 30-second spoken version ("tell me about yourself"). If the CV's story and the interview story diverge, the CV is wrong.
- Frame moves as deliberate. "Moved into operations to see whether my analyses survived contact with real processes" is a narrative; "then I got a job in operations" is a shrug. Use the summary and cover letter for this framing — the CV itself shows it through what you emphasise.

## Reframing, reordering, foregrounding, dropping

- **Reframe** = choose which true facet of a role to describe. A lab manager role is "scientific research" to one reader and "budget, scheduling, supplier management, and compliance" to another. Both are true; pick the facet the target rewards. Use the target field's vocabulary for the candidate's actual activities: "designed experiments" → "designed A/B-style controlled comparisons"; "wrote papers" → "communicated complex quantitative findings to non-specialist audiences" — only where the underlying activity genuinely matches.
- **Reorder** = control first impressions. Within a role, lead with on-thread bullets. Across the CV, put the section that carries the thread highest (often a skills/evidence block or a fresh qualification, not the latest job title).
- **Foreground** = spend lines proportional to relevance, not seniority or duration. A 6-month on-thread project can outrank a 4-year off-thread job for space.
- **Drop** = omit entirely only when it doesn't create a misleading gap. Omitting a 3-month temp job from 12 years ago: fine. Omitting the last two years because they're off-thread: not fine — compress them to one honest line instead. Omission that changes the reader's factual picture of the timeline is deception; omission of detail within an acknowledged role is editing.
- Job titles: never change them. If an internal title is opaque ("Programme Associate II"), add a truthful gloss in brackets: "Programme Associate II (data & reporting lead for 6-site trial)".

## Transferable-skills mapping

Do this mechanically, don't vibe it:
1. List the target role's required skills (from the ad) in a column.
2. For each, search the whole history — jobs, research, volunteering, side projects — for the strongest concrete instance.
3. Write that instance as an evidence bullet using the rules in cv-craft.md (verb + method + quantified outcome), in the target field's language.
4. Anything with no instance goes on the candidate's learning list — it does NOT go on the CV.

Translation discipline: a transferable skill claim must survive the "so what, prove it" test. "Strong analytical skills from my PhD" fails. "Analysed 4 years of field-trial data (10k+ observations) in R, including mixed-effects modelling" passes — the reader concludes "analytical skills" themselves. Always show the instance, never the label alone.

Watch for the classic hidden transferables in research/academic backgrounds: statistics and programming, experiment design, messy real-world data wrangling, literature synthesis (→ research and due diligence), teaching (→ training and presenting), grant writing (→ persuasive proposals, budget ownership), lab/fieldwork logistics (→ operations, supplier and stakeholder management), peer review (→ QA, giving structured feedback).

## Gaps and career changes — honest handling

- Account for every gap longer than ~3 months. An unexplained hole reads worse than almost any true explanation; recruiters assume the worst.
- One line, no apology, no essay: "Career break — parental leave (2022–2023)"; "Career break — relocation to the UK and full-time caring responsibility". Place it in the timeline like a role entry.
- Health: "Planned career break for a health matter, since resolved" is sufficient. The candidate owes no detail and employers may not demand it.
- Redundancy: attribute it to the role, not the person: "Role made redundant in company-wide restructure."
- Fill gaps with anything true and productive: courses, certifications, freelance work, volunteering, a personal project — these become bullets, and the gap becomes an entry.
- Date formats: month + year is the honest default. Years-only is acceptable house style applied consistently, but never adopt it specifically to bury a gap — if a gap only disappears under years-only dating, that's concealment.
- Career change ≠ gap. A change needs no excuse anywhere on the CV; it needs a summary that makes the destination look like the point of the journey (next section).

## The bridging summary

For a multi-track candidate the summary is the load-bearing wall: it's where the reader is told what the story is before they can invent a worse one ("job-hopper", "failed academic", "overqualified").

Formula: **[Identity claimed in TARGET terms] + [the thread: what the tracks share, with proof] + [why each major track is an asset for this role] + [the aim].**

Rules:
- Claim the target identity in the first three words if defensible ("Data analyst with…"), backed by real evidence. If not yet defensible, claim the capability: "Quantitative researcher moving into commercial analytics, with…".
- Name the track-switch as an asset, not a confession. Each prior track contributes one named strength.
- Never write "seeking to leverage my diverse background" — say what the diversity IS and what it buys the employer.
- The headline line can do heavy lifting when the last job title misleads: "Data Analyst | Python · R · SQL | 6 yrs experimental & operational data".

## Layout choice for career-changers

- **Reverse-chronological** (default): use when the thread is visible in the last 1–2 roles anyway. ~90% of recruiters prefer it; it is never a wrong answer.
- **Hybrid** (recommended for most career-changers): reverse-chronological history kept intact and dated, but preceded by a strong summary plus a "Key skills" or "Selected relevant experience/projects" evidence block that carries the thread. The reader gets the story first and the verifiable timeline second. This is the format to reach for in this skill.
- **Pure skills-based/functional** (skills sections replace the dated history): avoid. Recruiters read it as concealment — of gaps, of thin experience, of the timeline — and many ATS parse it badly. Use only when a chronological story is genuinely unusable (e.g. long absence from the workforce with rich untimed experience), and even then keep a minimal dated employment list at the bottom.

## Worked example — plant-science researcher → data analyst

History (all true): PhD + postdoc in plant science (7 yrs: field trials, R statistics, imaging data); 18 months as a data scientist at an agritech start-up (Python, dashboards; company folded); 2 years as operations coordinator at a food-logistics firm (scheduling, KPI reporting, process improvement). Target: **Data Analyst** at a mid-size commercial firm.

**Red thread**: a decade of turning messy real-world data into decisions people act on — in the field, in a start-up, and on the warehouse floor. NOT "plant scientist who drifted"; the operations role is reframed as the place the analyses met reality, not a detour.

**Layout**: hybrid. Summary → Key skills → Experience (reverse-chronological, all roles kept and dated) → Education. Education stays below experience — the PhD supports the story but must not lead it, or the CV reads as an academic's.

**Bridging summary**:
> Data analyst with 10 years' experience turning complex operational and experimental data into decisions, across research, agritech, and logistics. Built Python dashboards used daily by 25 growers; designed and analysed field trials (10k+ observations, R); as operations coordinator, built the KPI reporting that supported a 15% cut in delivery delays. Now seeking a full-time analyst role applying this end-to-end skill set commercially.

**Reframing per role** (facts unchanged, facet chosen for the thread):
- *Operations coordinator* — foreground the analytical facet, compress the routine: "Built weekly KPI dashboards in Excel/Power BI tracking on-time delivery across 3 depots; analysis informed route changes that cut delays 15%." The scheduling grind gets one line.
- *Agritech data scientist* — the strongest on-thread role: give it the most bullets despite being neither longest nor latest. Company folding is not explained on the CV (dates speak; cover letter or interview if asked): "Developed crop-yield prediction dashboards (Python/pandas/Plotly) used daily by 25 client growers."
- *PhD/postdoc* — translate outputs into analyst language: "Designed and analysed multi-season field trials (10k+ observations; mixed-effects models in R)"; "Presented findings to non-specialist funders and industry partners"; "Published 6 peer-reviewed papers" compressed to one line — publication count is off-thread, the analysis behind it is on-thread. Drop: taxonomy expertise, conference organising, teaching load detail (unless the target values training).

**What this example is NOT allowed to do**: retitle the operations job "Operations Analyst"; blur the agritech dates to hide the company's collapse; claim SQL because analysts need it if the candidate never used it. The candidate's real history, aimed — that is the whole method.
